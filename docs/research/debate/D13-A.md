# D13-A (FOR): Pexels in the visual fallback chain

## Thesis
Keep Pexels as the last-resort and intro/abstract layer. The alternative to a QA-failed scene is a text-only card, which is exactly the "templated, mass-produced" look R1 warns about (Plan.md R1; addendum G1, 11-addendum-automation-gaps.md line 11). Pexels costs $0, and the risks are small and can be controlled.

## Arguments
1. **Cost and quota fit.** Default limit is 200 req/hour and 20,000/month, and projects meeting the API terms can get unlimited requests free (https://www.pexels.com/api/documentation/, fetched). A 24 h cache makes a daily channel use a tiny fraction of that. Budget impact is $0 against the $30 cap.
2. **Commercial use is explicitly allowed.** Photos and videos are free for commercial use, attribution not required, modification allowed (https://www.pexels.com/license/, fetched). No clause there bans automated or AI-narrated channels.
3. **Attribution is cheap.** API docs ask for a prominent Pexels link and photographer credit "when possible" (API docs, fetched). Appending credit lines to the description is already planned (addendum section 5 item 7), and over-crediting is harmless.
4. **It beats the realistic alternative.** The addendum's own pipeline falls back to "Pexels B-roll + captions" when a scene fails QA (11-addendum lines 85, 95). A moving image under narration holds retention better than a static title card. That is my judgment, not measured (UNVERIFIED).
5. **Policy risk is manageable.** YouTube's reused-content policy turns on "significant original commentary, substantive changes, or educational value" (https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392, fetched). Original AI-written narration, code-drawn Manim/HTML scenes, and sources already supply that. Stock is used only for a minority of scenes. The page does not mention stock footage by name, so there is no explicit ban.

## Strongest opposing point and answer
Opposition: stock B-roll under tech narration is generic, can look irrelevant, and a channel of mostly stock plus TTS is the classic "inauthentic content" pattern.
Answer: agreed that heavy use would hurt. The proposition limits stock to intro/abstract scenes and failed-QA fallback, and the Haiku frame check still runs on it. Code-drawn visuals remain the backbone. A failed-QA scene can be rare, and when it happens the choice is stock versus a text card. The Pexels license also bars showing identifiable people in a bad light and implying brand endorsement (license page, fetched). Query filters (abstract, technology, no "people") and an LLM vision check on the frame cover this.

## What would change my mind
- Pexels replying (Spike G) that a YouTube description credit does NOT meet the API attribution rule (UNVERIFIED; help.pexels.com article returned 403 to me, so the addendum's secondary claims are unconfirmed).
- Pilot data showing fallback scenes exceed about 20% of runtime, or irrelevant-footage QA rejections above about 30%.
- Evidence of demonetization of automated channels using stock footage under the inauthentic-content policy (none found).
- Terms revision banning automated or AI-channel use.

## Scores (1-5, 5 = best/easiest)
- Cost: 5
- Output quality: 3
- Policy risk: 3 (5 = lowest risk; stock is allowed but adds modest R1 exposure)
- Implementation effort: 4
- Maintainability: 4
